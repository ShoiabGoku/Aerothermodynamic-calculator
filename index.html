Three ideas behind every tab

Get these and the rest of the calculator reads like plain English.

1

Mach number = speed ÷ speed of sound

Sound travels at about 340 m/s near the ground. M < 1 subsonic · M > 1 supersonic · M > 5 hypersonic. Below about M 0.3 air behaves like water (incompressible) — the world most civil and hydraulic formulas live in.

2

Stopping fast air heats it up

Kinetic energy turns back into heat. Air at 15 °C moving at Mach 2 reaches about 245 °C when brought to rest; at Mach 7, about 2,840 °C (ideal gas). That's why spacecraft need heat shields.

3

Shock waves are sudden jumps

When air can't get out of the way in time, its pressure, temperature and density jump across a layer thinner than a hair. A sonic boom and an explosion's blast wave are both shock waves.

ℹ️ About these models — the fidelity ladder

What it is — A ladder of increasing physical fidelity for air behind a hypersonic shock. Each rung adds one effect, so the difference between two rungs isolates that effect: ideal→real = molecular vibration; real→3-species = O₂ dissociation; 3→4 = N₂ dissociation; 4→5 = NO / Zeldovich chemistry.

How it's used here — All selected models solve the same conservation-based normal shock, so differences are pure physics, not numerics. The result cards give a one-line brief of each. Below the comparison you also get the nonequilibrium relaxation card (finite-rate Park chemistry — the path between frozen and equilibrium, matching a Laminar Finite-Rate CFD) and the two-temperature Park model (separate translational T and vibrational Tᵥ right behind the shock).

Where it's used — Re-entry capsules and hypersonic vehicle nose/leading-edge heating, TPS sizing, and CFD verification — the equilibrium models bound a nonequilibrium CFD, and the relaxation profiles say how close your Fluent run should sit to each bound.

Isentropic flow relations

How a gas's pressure, temperature and density change when it speeds up or slows down smoothly — through a nozzle, a pipe, a diffuser or a pitot tube. Type any one number and every other quantity follows.

Perfect gas, γ from the bar above. Enter any one quantity to get all the others; A/A* has separate subsonic and supersonic branches.
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — Isentropic flow means adiabatic and reversible — no shocks, no friction, no heat addition — so the entropy (and the total/stagnation temperature and pressure) stay constant. As a perfect gas speeds up or slows down, only its static properties change, tied to the Mach number.

How it's used here — The isentropic relations connect M to the ratios T₀/T, p₀/p, ρ₀/ρ and the area ratio A/A*. Enter any one of these (or a Mach/Prandtl–Meyer angle) and the tool inverts the relation to recover M and all the rest.

Where it's used — The backbone of compressible internal flow: sizing convergent–divergent nozzles and diffusers, wind-tunnel design, reducing pitot/total-pressure probe data, and converting between static and stagnation conditions anywhere the flow is smooth.

Normal shock relations

A shock wave standing straight across a supersonic flow: the air slows down abruptly while its pressure, temperature and density jump — inside a layer thinner than a human hair. Sonic booms, blast waves and engine inlets all work this way.

Perfect gas, γ from the bar. The upstream flow must be supersonic (M₁ ≥ 1).
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — A normal shock is a very thin (a few mean-free-paths) compression wave standing perpendicular to a supersonic stream. Across it the flow jumps abruptly to subsonic, with sudden rises in pressure, temperature and density and an unavoidable loss of total pressure (entropy increases — the process is irreversible).

How it's used here — The Rankine–Hugoniot relations link the upstream and downstream states through M₁ alone. The tool solves from any one quantity (M₁, M₂, or any ratio) and also reports the pitot ratio p₀₂/p₁.

Where it's used — Supersonic engine inlets (the terminal/normal shock that decelerates flow before the compressor), pitot probes in supersonic flow (Rayleigh–Pitot), shock tubes, over-expanded rocket nozzles, and the blunt-body bow shock right on the stagnation streamline.

Oblique shock — wedge / compression ramp

When supersonic air meets a ramp or wedge it is turned through an angled shock wave. Find the shock angle, how strongly the air is compressed, and the largest turn before the shock pops off the nose.

Real-gas oblique resolves the shock-normal Mach into the equilibrium normal-shock solver (tangential velocity preserved).
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — An oblique shock is a shock inclined at an angle β to the flow, formed when a supersonic stream is turned into itself by a wedge or ramp (deflection θ). It is weaker than a normal shock and the flow usually stays supersonic behind it. Only the velocity component normal to the shock is compressed; the tangential component passes through unchanged.

How it's used here — The θ–β–M relation ties deflection, wave angle and Mach together; the normal component obeys the normal-shock relations. There are two solutions (weak, usually seen; strong), and above a maximum θ the shock detaches into a bow shock. The gas-model selector lets you run it as a perfect gas or feed the normal Mach into the real-gas / 5-species equilibrium solver.

Where it's used — Supersonic inlet compression ramps and mixed-compression inlets (scramjets), wedge and cone leading edges, deflected control surfaces, and shock-wave / boundary-layer and shock–shock interactions.

Conical shock — Taylor–Maccoll (perfect gas)

Supersonic flow over a pointed cone — a rocket nose, a missile or a bullet tip. Find where the shock sits and how much pressure the cone surface feels.

Sharp cone at zero incidence. The shock angle is found by shooting the Taylor–Maccoll ODE; surface flow is uniform along rays.
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — The axisymmetric counterpart of the wedge: supersonic flow over a sharp cone. Because the flow can spread three-dimensionally around the cone, the shock lies closer to the body than a wedge shock of the same angle, and the region between the shock and the cone is non-uniform — the flow keeps compressing as it approaches the surface.

How it's used here — A conical shock angle is guessed; the Taylor–Maccoll ordinary differential equation is integrated (RK4) inward from just behind the shock until the flow becomes parallel to the cone surface. The shock angle is found by shooting so that this happens exactly at the cone half-angle.

Where it's used — Missile and rocket nose cones, supersonic projectiles, conical forebodies and inlet centre-bodies, and any pointed axisymmetric body in supersonic flight.

Prandtl–Meyer expansion fan

The opposite of a shock: when supersonic air turns away around a corner it smoothly speeds up, and its pressure and temperature drop — with no energy lost.

Isentropic expansion through angle Δθ (perfect gas, γ from bar): ν(M₂) = ν(M₁) + Δθ.
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — A Prandtl–Meyer expansion is the smooth, isentropic acceleration of a supersonic flow as it turns around a convex corner — the exact opposite of a shock. The flow fans out through a continuous set of Mach waves; Mach number rises while pressure, temperature and density fall. Because it is isentropic, no total pressure is lost.

How it's used here — The Prandtl–Meyer function ν(M) measures how much a flow has expanded. It is simply additive: ν(M₂) = ν(M₁) + Δθ. From M₂ the isentropic relations give the p, T, ρ ratios across the fan.

Where it's used — Flow expanding around the shoulder/afterbody of a vehicle, the suction (lee) side of supersonic airfoils and wedges, under- and over-expanded nozzle plumes, and expansion corners in supersonic ducts — often paired with oblique shocks in shock-expansion theory.

US Standard Atmosphere 1976

Temperature, pressure, density and speed of sound at any height up to 86 km — or work backwards from a measured pressure, density or temperature to the altitude. Enter a wind speed to get the wind pressure on a structure.

Valid to 86 km geometric. Go either way: altitude → properties, or enter a pressure / density / temperature to recover the altitude.
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — The US Standard Atmosphere 1976 is an internationally-agreed, idealized model of how the still, average atmosphere's temperature and pressure vary with altitude, built as a stack of layers with constant temperature lapse rates.

How it's used here — Geometric altitude is converted to geopotential altitude, then each layer's barometric formula gives T and p; density, speed of sound and Sutherland viscosity follow. It also runs in reverse: pressure and density fall monotonically with height, so either gives a single altitude (how a pressure altimeter works); temperature is not monotonic, so a given T can match several altitudes and whole isothermal layers — all matches are listed.

Where it's used — Defining the freestream (T∞, p∞, ρ∞) for flight at a given altitude, converting a measured pressure/density back to an altitude, re-entry/ascent trajectory analysis, calibrating altimeters, and standardizing aircraft and engine performance data so results are comparable.

Trajectory / parameter sweep — stagnation heating & shock

Run the heating calculation across a range of speeds, Mach numbers or altitudes and see, as curves, how nose temperature, heat flux and shock stand-off change along a flight path.

Sweeps the aerothermo solver to plot stagnation T₀, heat flux (Fay–Riddell & Sutton–Graves) and shock stand-off — e.g. along a re-entry trajectory.
ℹ️ About this tool — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — Not a physics model on its own, but a driver that runs the Aerothermo bow-shock solver many times while marching one variable (velocity, Mach or altitude) across a range — so you see a curve rather than a single point.

How it's used here — At each swept value it builds the freestream and solves the chosen gas model, plotting stagnation temperature T₀, the Fay–Riddell and Sutton–Graves heat fluxes, and the shock stand-off.

Where it's used — Mapping the aeroheating envelope along a re-entry or ascent trajectory, locating the peak-heating point that sizes the thermal-protection system, and comparing how much hotter/cooler a vehicle gets across a speed or altitude band.

Shock tube / reflected-shock tunnel

High-pressure gas on one side of a thin diaphragm, low-pressure gas on the other. Burst it and a shock wave races down the tube — the laboratory's way of making shock waves, blast loads and very hot gas on demand. Every wave is drawn on an x–t diagram.

Ideal (perfect-gas) shock tube. Burst the diaphragm: an incident shock runs into the driven gas, a contact surface and an expansion fan form; the shock reflects off the closed end to make the high-enthalpy reservoir (region 5) that feeds the nozzle. Set the driver pressure high enough that p₄ > p₁.
ℹ️ About this model — what it is · how it's used · where

What it is — A shock tube is a tube split by a diaphragm into a high-pressure driver gas and a low-pressure driven (test) gas. When the diaphragm bursts, a shock wave sweeps into the driven gas while an expansion fan runs back into the driver — a simple, impulsive way to make a short slug of very hot, fast-moving gas. In a reflected-shock tunnel the shock bounces off the closed end and brings the gas to rest, creating a small high-temperature, high-pressure reservoir that is then expanded through a nozzle to hypersonic test conditions.

How it's used here — The shock-tube equation gives the incident shock strength from the diaphragm pressure ratio; the incident shock, contact surface, expansion fan and reflected shock are then solved in closed form, and an optional nozzle expands the reservoir to a test-section freestream. The x–t diagram shows every wave.

Where it's used — Ground testing of hypersonic vehicles and re-entry conditions, measuring high-temperature chemical-kinetic rates and radiation, and TPS / scramjet testing in reflected-shock tunnels (e.g. T4, HIEST, LENS, HEG).

📘 Equations & method used (click to expand) — full derivation chain

Stations. 4 = driver (high-p), 1 = driven (low-p test gas); after the diaphragm bursts: 2 = behind the incident shock, 3 = expanded driver gas (2 & 3 are separated by the contact surface, with p₃ = p₂ and u₃ = u₂), 5 = gas processed by the reflected shock (the stagnant high-enthalpy reservoir). γ₁,R₁ are the driven-gas properties; γ₄,R₄ the driver. All relations below are exactly what the calculator evaluates.

1 · Gas state

a = √(γ R T)     ρ = p / (R T)   speed of sound, density (each gas)

2 · Diaphragm (shock-tube) equation → incident shock strength

Given the diaphragm pressure ratio p₄/p₁, the incident-shock pressure ratio P ≡ p₂/p₁ is the root of:

p₄/p₁ = P · [ 1 − (γ₄−1)(a₁/a₄)(P−1) ⁄ √( 2γ₁ ( 2γ₁ + (γ₁+1)(P−1) ) ) ] −2γ₄ ⁄ (γ₄−1)

Solved numerically for P = p₂/p₁ (bisection). It encodes “shock compression in the driven gas = isentropic expansion in the driver, matched in p and u at the contact surface.”

3 · Incident shock (driven gas, γ₁)

Ms = √[ (γ₁+1)/(2γ₁) · (p₂/p₁ − 1) + 1 ]     Ws = Ms a₁   shock Mach & lab-frame speed
T₂/T₁ = [ 2γ₁Ms² − (γ₁−1) ][ (γ₁−1)Ms² + 2 ] ⁄ [ (γ₁+1)² Ms² ]
ρ₂/ρ₁ = (γ₁+1)Ms² ⁄ [ (γ₁−1)Ms² + 2 ]     a₂ = a₁ √(T₂/T₁)
u₂ = (2a₁)/(γ₁+1) · ( Ms − 1/Ms )   induced (contact / mass) velocity

4 · Contact surface & expansion fan (driver gas, γ₄)

p₃ = p₂ ,   u₃ = u₂   matched across the contact surface
T₃/T₄ = (p₃/p₄)(γ₄−1)/γ₄     a₃ = a₄ (p₃/p₄)(γ₄−1)/2γ₄
u₃ = (2a₄)/(γ₄−1) · [ 1 − (p₃/p₄)(γ₄−1)/2γ₄ ]   (= u₂; this closes the diaphragm equation)

Centred-fan speeds (lab frame): head = −a₄, tail = u₃ − a₃. Inside the fan, with ξ = x/t:

u(ξ) = 2/(γ₄+1) · (ξ + a₄) ,   a = a₄ − (γ₄−1)/2 · u ,   p = p₄ (a/a₄)2γ₄/(γ₄−1) ,   T = T₄ (a/a₄)²

5 · Reflected shock → reservoir (region 5, u₅ = 0)

The shock reflects off the closed end and brings the gas to rest. Requiring u₅ = 0 gives, in closed form,

K = u₂ (γ₁+1) / (2a₂) ,   Mr = ( K + √(K²+4) ) / 2   reflected-shock Mach (rel. to gas 2)
p₅/p₂ = 1 + 2γ₁/(γ₁+1) · (Mr² − 1)
T₅/T₂ = [ 2γ₁Mr² − (γ₁−1) ][ (γ₁−1)Mr² + 2 ] ⁄ [ (γ₁+1)² Mr² ]
ρ₅/ρ₂ = (γ₁+1)Mr² ⁄ [ (γ₁−1)Mr² + 2 ]     Wr = Mr a₂ − u₂  (reflected-shock speed)

6 · Nozzle → test section (reservoir 5 ≈ stagnation, isentropic)

Ae/A* → Me (supersonic root) ,   Te = T₅ ⁄ (1 + ½(γ₁−1)Me²) ,   pe = p₅ ⁄ (1 + ½(γ₁−1)Me²)γ₁/(γ₁−1)
Ve = Me √(γ₁ R₁ Te) ,   ρe = pe/(R₁ Te) ,   Re/m = ρe Ve / μ(Te)

7 · x–t wave diagram (each wave is x = c·t)

Incident shock c = Ws; contact surface c = u₂; fan head c = −a₄, tail c = u₃−a₃ (a fan of rays in between); reflected shock leaves the wall at (x=L₁, t=L₁/Ws) with speed −Wr until it meets the contact surface.

Assumptions

Ideal calorically-perfect gases (constant γ, R per gas); instantaneous diaphragm rupture; 1-D inviscid flow; no boundary-layer attenuation of the shock; no real-gas / vibrational / dissociation effects in the hot reservoir (for those, hand T₅,p₅ as a freestream into the Aerothermo tab). Refs: Anderson, Modern Compressible Flow; Gaydon & Hurle, The Shock Tube in High-Temperature Chemical Physics.
